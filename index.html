How does the radiation on Mars change with place and time?

Mars Radiation Explorer turns published Curiosity RAD measurements into a model that scientists can adjust, check against the data, and map across the planet.

The problem we are solving

Radiation is one of the biggest health risks for people on Mars, yet it is hard to understand how it varies.

PROBLEM 1

One site, one instrument

The surface dose measurements used here come from Curiosity’s RAD instrument at Gale Crater. Nobody has measured the dose at other places.

PROBLEM 2

Results are scattered

Findings sit in separate papers, with different units (absorbed dose in µGy, dose equivalent in µSv) and different detectors.

PROBLEM 3

Unclear where models apply

It is hard to see where measurements end and extrapolation begins, for example in deep basins or on high volcanoes.

PROBLEM 4

Far from decisions

The link from a dose rate per day to a whole-mission dose and its limits is rarely shown in one place.

Our answer: one explorer where you change the atmosphere, the Sun and the site; see the dose rate respond with uncertainty; check the model against RAD; map where it is valid; and see what it means for a crew.

Cosmic rays: steady

≈ 220 µGy/day

High-energy particles from deep space reach the surface all the time. At Gale the average absorbed dose rate is about 220 µGy per day.

The atmosphere: a shield

22 g/cm² vs 1,000

Mars has a thin atmosphere (about 21–23 g/cm² at Gale, against about 1,000 g/cm² on Earth) and no global magnetic field. When pressure rises the dose falls.

Solar storms: sudden

25–40 mSv per event

Bursts of particles from the Sun add dose fast. An example surface event gives 40 mSv in the open or 25 mSv under 10 cm of water-equivalent shielding.

What we do not know yet

A good model shows its limits. These are the open questions this tool makes visible.

Measured at one place

  • Surface dose comes from Gale only, over a limited pressure range.
  • Other sites rely on the pressure relation.

Valid only in a range

  • The straight-line relation is fitted to 700–950 Pa.
  • The authors warn against extrapolating it to the top of the atmosphere.
  • High volcanoes are far outside the range.

Not in the model

  • Latitude, soil composition and underground shielding.
  • Detector and tissue differences in absorbed dose.
  • Solar storms, which are random and hard to forecast.

Where data would help

  • Dose measurements at a second site, such as a deep basin.
  • Real elevation data (MOLA) in the map.
  • Dose at different depths in regolith.

Sourced

  • 220 µGy/day average, pressure slope, 700–950 Pa range
  • Quality factor 3.05 and 1.8 mSv/day in cruise
  • 600 mSv and 250 mSv limits, storm examples

Modelled

  • Pressure from elevation (11 km scale height)
  • Dose at other places and times of day
  • Dose map and mission totals

Assumed

  • 840 Pa as the typical Gale pressure for the 220 µGy/day point
  • Caution zone limits (300–1,500 Pa)
  • Habitat shielding and storm timing

Words you will see
Absorbed dose (µGy)
Energy absorbed per kilogram. Measured by the detector.
Dose equivalent (µSv, mSv)
Absorbed dose weighted by how harmful the particles are. Used for health limits.
Quality factor (Q)
The weight that turns absorbed dose into dose equivalent. About 3 on the Mars surface.
Column depth (g/cm²)
The mass of air above each square centimetre. More air means more shielding.
Solar modulation (Φ)
How strongly the Sun’s activity pushes cosmic rays away. Measured in megavolts (MV).
RAD
The Radiation Assessment Detector on Curiosity.
